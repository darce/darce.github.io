Visual Search Workbench lets you find photographs in your own folders and explore where they lead. Start with words or a reference picture, inspect the results, then make a promising image the next search. A similarity map offers another route through the collection, without requiring you to tag the photographs first.

I designed and built the app to explore this movement between finding a picture and developing a visual direction. The app runs on Apple Silicon Macs, keeps image processing on-device, and has been tested on an 8 GB machine.

![A search for flowers returns photographs from a film-scan collection, with a selected image open for inspection.](/images/work/sis-search-layer.png)

*These application captures show version 0.2.0, under the earlier name Semantic Image Search. The downloadable release documented here is 0.2.3.*

## Give the search a starting point

The search begins with the folders you choose. The app reads their photographs into a separate search index and leaves the originals where they are. That index lets you search by what a picture depicts even when its filename is a camera number.

You can give the search two kinds of query: a description or a reference picture. A description is useful when you remember a subject but not the filename. A reference picture is useful when showing what interests you is easier than putting it into words.

Drop a reference picture into the app, or choose **Find similar** on a photograph in the collection. That photograph becomes the query for the next set of results. A banner names the reference, and **Back to text results** returns to the earlier query.

![A flower photograph is named as the reference for Find similar, above the resulting images.](/images/work/sis-similarity-search.png)

A result can therefore become a new direction. You might begin by looking for flowers and continue because a particular image suggests something useful for a colour study. That is the kind of exploration I wanted the app to support.

## Explore the collection on a map

The **Similarity** view arranges related images on a map using UMAP. UMAP takes the images’ numerical representations and looks for a layout that keeps neighbours near one another. The result gives you regions to explore instead of a list to read from top to bottom.

A region becomes useful when you can see the photographs behind it. Select a region, and its pictures appear in the linked grid. The grid lets you judge the images themselves and choose another one to follow.

![A Similarity map of a 2,314-photo collection, with photographs from a selected region shown in the grid.](/images/work/sis-layers-umap.png)

*Selecting a region connects the overview to the photographs it contains.*

The map suggests where to look. Its groups are not automatically named categories, and the gaps between them are not exact measures of visual difference. [Finding a Visual Direction with UMAP](/research/visual-search-workbench-process/) explains the method and the design idea; the [interaction reference](/research/visual-search-workbench-screen-reference/) follows the controls and views.

## Look at the selection by place or time

A selection can be explored through **Similarity**, **Places**, or **Timeline**. Similarity organises the images by relationships in their representations. Places uses recorded coordinates. Timeline uses dates to show broader or narrower periods.

The selection carries between those views, and each view retains its zoom and pan when you change tabs. You can examine the same material from another angle without rebuilding the view. Selecting an individual photograph opens its available file metadata, including camera information, capture time, and location.

## Take a cutout into another project

A useful photograph may contain a subject you want to use in a composition. The app’s experimental cutout feature proposes subject layers with transparent backgrounds. Those layers can be inspected and saved for use elsewhere.

![A cat photograph with proposed cutout layers, each marked experimental.](/images/work/sis-cat-layers-detail.png)

The layers still require judgment: an edge can be rough, a label can be wrong, or the proposed separation may not suit the composition. Cutouts remain a secondary experiment alongside search and exploration.

## Keep the work on the Mac

The app bundles the search and cutout models, so they are available without a first-launch model download. The models process the photographs locally. My network checks found no gallery data leaving the device in the tested flows.

The 8 GB test establishes a working configuration rather than a memory requirement for every library. Larger collections still need measurement, and an outside-user study has not yet run. That study needs to examine both finding a remembered image and discovering useful material for a creative brief.
