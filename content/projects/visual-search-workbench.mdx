Imagine looking for a flower photo in a folder of film scans. You remember the pink petals, but the filename is just a number. You could open each scan, or describe what you remember and start from the closest results. Apple's Photo app helps, but not for images kept in random folders and drives.

Visual Search Workbench makes a different search path possible. It searches the photo folders you choose using words or a reference picture. From a promising result, you can find similar photos, explore the collection by location or date, and save matte cutouts for use elsewhere.

A reference picture can be the beginning of a moodboard, a colour study, or a composition. Following similar images lets you walk through the collection, even when you don't quite have the words to describe what you're looking for yet.

## Find a starting point

Start by choosing a folder. The app reads the photos where they are and builds a search index: a separate catalogue it can use to compare pictures. The originals stay in their folders, and the image processing runs on your Mac; nothing gets uploaded anywhere.

Once the folder is indexed, you can search for something like “flowers” or “red bicycle.” The app compares the meaning of the phrase with the pictures, so a useful result does not depend on someone having named or tagged the file.

Sometimes a picture is easier than a description. Drop a reference image onto the window, or choose **Find similar** on a photo you already have. The results now follow that picture. A banner names the reference image and gives you a way back to the text results, so you can tell which search you are looking at.

![Find similar uses a pink-flower photo as the reference, names it above the results, and offers a Back to text results button.](/images/work/sis-similarity-search.png)

These results are suggestions ordered by similarity. A high position does not prove that a picture contains what you asked for. The interface avoids turning similarity scores into confidence percentages, and it can show “No close matches” when the ranking gives too little useful distinction between results. The nearest photos remain available to inspect.

## Follow the pictures

A useful result can lead somewhere the original query did not. Perhaps a flower photo reminds you of a trip, or its colours suggest a direction for a design. You can explore those connections through **Similarity**, **Places**, and **Timeline**.

**Similarity** groups visually related photos on a map. You can select a cluster and inspect its pictures in the grid. The map is a way to browse neighbourhoods; the size of a gap between clusters is not a precise measure of how different the pictures are.

![The Similarity view groups a 2,314-photo library into a point cloud, with photos in the selected region shown as thumbnails.](/images/work/sis-layers-umap.png)

**Places** shows photos with recorded coordinates. Select a region to find pictures taken there. **Timeline** groups photos by date, letting you narrow a collection to a year, month, or smaller period. It distinguishes recorded capture dates from file dates and keeps undated photos visible as a separate count.

Your selection carries between these views. Their zoom and pan also survive tab changes, so you can examine the same set from another angle without rebuilding your view.

Selecting a photo opens its details, including the camera, capture time, and location when those are available. Those details give you another way to judge whether a suggested picture is the one you need.

## Take a cutout into another project : **experimental feature**

Once you find a useful picture, the app can separate subjects into layers with transparent backgrounds. You can inspect a layer and save it for composition elsewhere.

![A cat photo has three proposed cutout layers, labelled cat, plant, and left plant, each marked experimental.](/images/work/sis-cat-layers-detail.png)

These layers are labelled **experimental** because a proposed cutout still needs inspection. A recognisable subject does not guarantee a clean edge or a useful separation from its background. There is also a limit to what the local system can do in a reasonable time. Expect frayed edges, mislabeled objects, nonsensical segmentation. Still, this feature can do some of the legwork for you.

## What I have checked

The app has been tested on an 8 GB Apple Silicon Mac. Network checks confirm no gallery data leaving the device in the tested flows.

A task-based study with outside users has **not yet run**, and larger libraries remain unmeasured. The next evaluation needs to establish whether people can find useful photos and correctly interpret the missing-data explanations.

The [process article](/research/visual-search-workbench-process/) explains how I designed and reviewed those states. The [screen reference](/research/visual-search-workbench-screen-reference/) preserves the detailed drawings and release-specific behaviour.
