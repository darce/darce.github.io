I use the project record when the next coding decision depends on work outside the current conversation. The record can bring back an earlier rationale or show that a new-looking failure has already occurred across several tasks.

Five questions recur in my use of [WorkBay](/projects/workbay/): **where trouble repeats**, **whether a fix exists**, **why an approach was chosen**, **what supports completion**, and **what has already failed**. Each question calls for a different part of the record.

The examples below are field observations from my development practice, not a controlled productivity study. [Project Memory Outside the Conversation](/research/agent-memory/) explains the storage and retrieval mechanism.

## Where does trouble repeat?

A review usually concerns the work in front of it. A sequence of reviews can reveal trouble that keeps returning across tasks. The project record makes that sequence available even when no single session has seen all of it.

In one earlier audit, roughly three quarters of the recorded reviews in a small project had failed. That pattern prompted a closer look at the architecture. The count did not establish the cause: task selection and review practice could also have affected the proportion.

The coral cluster illustrates what the wider view makes visible: repeated failures that a single session might miss. The findings behind the count were the material to inspect. Their shared constraints could suggest a broader problem, while differences between them could show that the apparent pattern had several causes. The count directed the investigation; the findings had to support the diagnosis.

## Does a useful fix already exist?

A new finding may resemble a problem recorded under different wording. Semantic retrieval can surface an earlier resolution, which a human or agent can compare with the current code.

The resolution may contain more than a patch. It can preserve the constraint that made the patch necessary or the reason a simpler change failed. That explanation helps determine whether the earlier fix applies now.

An earlier fix remains a candidate until its conditions and recorded status have been checked. Copying the patch without that check would replace repeated investigation with a different risk: applying the right answer to the wrong problem.

## Why was this approach chosen?

A decision record preserves the reasons behind a choice. Those reasons give a later discussion a starting point even when the people or tools involved were absent from the original conversation.

For example, a later session may question why a site uses static output rather than server rendering. The recorded rationale can identify the requirements the choice addressed. Those requirements can then be compared with the needs of the current work.

A changed requirement may justify a changed decision. The record should make that change deliberate, not keep the original choice closed forever. Its value is distinguishing a new reason from an old debate whose reasoning has been forgotten.

## What supports the completion claim?

A completion claim needs evidence tied to the work being assessed. WorkBay can preserve review verdicts and test records alongside task and commit information. That information lets another reviewer inspect which commands ran, what they returned, and which revision they concern.

The revision matters because a test result from earlier code does not automatically establish the state of the current code. A useful review follows the evidence back to the change instead of relying on a summary saying that the task passed.

Another model can perform that review without inheriting the author’s conversation. Semantic search may help locate the relevant discussion, but the completion assessment depends on the exact records and the code.

## What has already failed?

A failed approach leaves information that can disappear from a recap of the eventual success. Blockers and abandoned attempts preserve that information, including why the approach was set aside.

The reason distinguishes an option that was temporarily unavailable from one that produced incorrect results. An unavailable dependency may later become usable. An incorrect result may expose a constraint that still applies.

The history therefore does not forbid another attempt. It makes the next attempt answer a specific question: what has changed since this failed?

## From a useful lookup to an evaluated benefit

These questions give the record a practical role in the next decision. The examples show what can be inspected; they do not establish how often retrieval finds the right material or how much work it saves.

That benefit needs a comparison that measures both effort and the resulting work. [Testing Whether Project Memory Saves Work](/research/economics-of-agent-memory/) sets out the proposed comparison, keeping the evaluation separate from these observations of use.
